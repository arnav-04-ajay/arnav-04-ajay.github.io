Project Overview

Catch-and-release fishing is a popular hobby that allows people to experience the joy of fishing while also being environmentally sustainable. However, many casual catch-and-release fishermen might not be familiar with the best practices to follow to ensure the fish gets released safely. The NOAA recommends that the fish have minimal exposure to air (under 60 seconds if possible), minimal physical handling of the fish, and to avoid touching the gills and eyes of the fish. If these guidelines aren’t followed, the fish’s rate of survival once released declines, and thus we aimed to create a device that would eliminate the need to directly handle the fish while also passing water over the fish’s gills to help resuscitate it during release.

Project Goals

Power and Logic Circuitry

Design of a circuit to power and control all components required for live video feed and fish detection, water pumps, and a release mechanism.

Ease of Use

Wanted the product to be relatively hands off during operation but also have some optional controls for the user.

Quality of Life Features

Implement an app to keep track of operation status and of what fish have been released previously.

Power Management and Operation Logic

The operation of this fish-release device required 3 main components:

  • The detection of the fish within the device itself
  • Water pumps to facilitate the flow of oxygen through the gills of the fish
  • A door release mechanism

Given these requirements, I designed a circuit to help power a Raspberry Pi 4B (which would be used process live video feed), an Arduino Nano (to toggle the door release and pumps independently), two pumps, a servo motor for the door release mechanism, and 3 indicator lights to tell the user if the system was online, if the door was open, and if the pumps were running respectively. The Raspberry Pi uses simple blob detection to determine when a fish is in the device and starts a timer, which keeps track of when to trigger the individual pumps and when to release the fish. These events are communicated to the Arduino Nano via GPIO pins, after which the Nano handles a state system using the given inputs to toggle the release door and turn the individual pumps on or off.

Live Video and Product App

We used a simple Arducam to take live video, which was streamed via a local network hosted by the Raspberry Pi. The app features a simple design made in flutter, and does the following:

  • Displays the live video feed
  • and displays a picture of each fish released (taken immediately after detection)
  • Has buttons that toggle endpoints on the Raspberry Pi (for manual door and pump toggling if desired)

Physical Design

Although I didn't directly deal with the physical design that much, the overall goal was to keep the design lightweight and for it to have a relatively small profile (overall weight was < 7lbs). A custom carbon fiber layup was done for the overall structure, and custom nozzles were made to assist with water flow out of the pumps.
